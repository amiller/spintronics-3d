# How Spintronics works

The physical mechanisms below refer to Upper Story's kit. The simulator equations refer to the checked-in official source, which Upper Story distributes under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. [Official simulator and source license](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/simulator/)

## Chain, sprockets, and units

Spintronics represents current with chain speed and voltage differences with differences in chain tension. Each span between parts corresponds to a wire. The two spans at a sprocket are its two terminals, and their tension difference produces the sprocket torque. A single chain around a battery and two resistors puts the resistors in series because the same length of chain moves past all three parts per second. A junction splits that flow into separate loops and combines their returns. A slack span is a convenient zero-tension reference. Routing a part outside a loop reverses its sign relative to a part inside it. On the bench, excessive chain tension adds friction, and stiction can change a capacitor's voltage reading. [Upper Story science guide](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science) · [Upper Story troubleshooting](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/troubleshooting/) · [Steve Mould's demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QrkiJZKJfpY) · [Video transcript, 0:39–1:30 and 5:01–6:16](https://lilys.ai/ko/notes/638148)

In kit units, 1 spin coulomb is 10 m of chain. Write $x=r\theta$ for signed chain travel in metres at pitch radius $r$, $v=r\omega$ for chain speed in m/s, and $\tau=r\Delta F$ for sprocket torque in N·m due to a tension difference in newtons. Then $Q=x/10$ in spin coulombs and $I=v/10$ in spin amps. From $VI=\Delta F\,v=\tau\omega$, it follows that $V=10\Delta F=10\tau/r$ in spin volts. Thus 1 spin volt corresponds to 0.1 N of tension difference. The physical chain pack lists about 1.92 m for 300 links, or about 6.4 mm per link. [Upper Story science guide and units table](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science) · [Chain product specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-chain-300-links)

The simulator stores a chain as an ordered list of `{partIndex, level, cw}` connections. The editor starts at a free sprocket (left of centre gives clockwise wrap), keeps subsequent picks on that level, and closes the loop by returning to the first sprocket with the matching wrap. Each level of a part takes one chain, and a loop needs at least two sprockets. UI levels 1, 2, and 3 correspond to indices 0, 1, and 2. Most parts assign these three levels to one shaft. A junction has three independent sprockets subject to its differential, and a transistor has two independent sprockets at levels 1 and 3. Crossing chains couple only through an explicitly shared mechanism. For a closed chain with $n$ sprockets, the simulator creates $n-1$ Planck gear joints, all referenced to the first connection, since the reference variable is never advanced. Set $s_i=+1$ for `cw=true` and $-1$ otherwise. With gear ratio $g_i=-s_0s_i r_i/r_0$, each joint imposes $\theta_0+g_i\theta_i=\mathrm{constant}$, so $s_0 r_0\omega_0=s_i r_i\omega_i$. Equal wraps give the same angular sign, and opposite wraps reverse it. These are ideal no-slip constraints within solver error. They impose neither a tension-only condition nor chain mass, slack, elasticity, tooth engagement, or friction. The visible loop is drawn with tangents and arcs, including the return to the first sprocket. Its reported length is the sum of the rendered segment and arc lengths divided by 2.842 px/mm. The separately defined `worldScale` is 2000 px/m and is used, for example, to position the interaction mouse joint. Gear ratios use each part's explicit physics radii in metres. ## Solver and simulator calibration

The editor loads Phaser 3, Planck.js v0.3.0-rc.2 and Tone.js. The sprockets use a zero-gravity Planck world. Each Phaser scene update calls `world.step(1/30)` once, clears forces, then runs the part updates. Torques set there act in the following step. That is 1/30 **simulated** second per scene update, with no elapsed-time argument or accumulator in the callback. The bundled world defaults give eight velocity iterations and up to three position iterations per island, with sleeping, warm starting, continuous collision detection and block solving enabled, and substepping disabled. Before updating position, the solver caps each body's translation at 2 m and rotation at $\pi/2$ rad per step, corresponding to 60 m/s and $15\pi\approx47.12$ rad/s here. The cap comes after velocity constraints and can leave post-step sprocket speeds out of ratio. Islands can sleep after 0.5 simulated seconds below the velocity tolerances of 0.01 m/s and 2°/s, once their position constraints are solved. Fixtures sharing filter group −1 suppress contacts with one another. [index.html:94–98](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/index.html#L94) · [planck.min.js:1–4](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.min.js#L1) · [spintronicssimulator.js:42–46,316–321,569–578](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/spintronicssimulator.js#L569) · [part-manager.js:43–48](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/part-manager.js#L43) · [planck.js:2675–2680,3328–3367,3734–3836,4253–4261,4846–4893](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.js#L4253)

Below, $\tau_P$ is torque in Planck's numeric units, $d$ is angular damping in inverse simulated seconds, and $\rho$ is fixture mass per model area. Each centred circular fixture of radius $r$ gives moment of inertia $J=\frac{\pi}{2}\rho r^4$ in model mass·m². For a dynamic rotor, with $h=1/30$, the solver first computes $\omega_*=(\omega_n+h\tau_P/J)/(1+hd)$, then applies constraint impulses. For a drag-only rotor held at steady speed by a gear joint, the joint must supply torque $Jd\omega/(1+hd)$, computed as its angular impulse per step divided by $h$. This differs from $Jd\omega$, the torque required when driving that same rotor via `applyTorque` before damping. [planck.js:3686–3709,3734–3744,10192–10222,14022–14026](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.js#L3686)

The radius and step support a kinematic conversion $I=r\omega/10$ in spin amps per the kit convention, using simulated seconds. For example, at $r=0.020584$ m, $1$ rad/s corresponds to $2.0584$ mA. A torque conversion has the form $V=10S\tau_P/r$, where $S\tau_P$ is physical N·m. The battery's N·m/1000 comment suggests $S=1000$, giving about **12.80 spin V** from its active constant and radius, while Upper Story rates the physical battery at 6 spin V. The capacitor and transistor spring constants include an explicit extra factor of 2.5. Removing that factor when comparing with physical values, or using $S=400$, gives 9 V at the capacitor's reference deflection for $C=1$ mF and 1.5 V of transistor spring torque at full gate travel, but only **5.12 spin V** for the simulated source. The capacitor reference displacement at that radius implies $Q/9\approx1.150$ mF. A single calibrated volts-to-torque scale for all parts is **unverified**. ## Parts

### Battery (`motor`)

The 6 spin V, 0.3 mAh kit battery stores energy in a constant-torque spring wound by the pull string. Gearing drives the output sprocket. A fast unloaded run, the mechanical short-circuit case, trips a seatbelt-like breaker. Upper Story's teardown shows the flyweight disc, cam, ratchet gear and pawl, consistent with a centrifugal trip and a mechanical latch. The precise trip speed and flyweight-to-pawl linkage are **unverified**. Pulling the string again resets the breaker. [Upper Story battery specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/products/spintronic-battery-7-v) · [Upper Story battery teardown](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/troubleshooting/) · [Mould transcript, 9:20–9:37](https://lilys.ai/ko/notes/638148)

The simulator applies `0.00001648875` directly to the output sprocket on every untripped update. The output, intermediate, and drive rotors use fixture radii $0.025771/2, 0.0364/2, 0.042/2$ m, respectively, all at `ρ=0.1` and initially `d=0.02`. The gear ratios are $(0.014/2)/(0.0364/2)=5/13$ and $(0.014/2)/(0.042/2)=1/3$, so the nominal speed relation is `ω_intermediate = −(5/13)ω_output` and `ω_drive = (5/39)ω_output` before any body clamp. Battery levels 1–3 share the output shaft. [parts/motor-part.js:5–9,155–194,260–282](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/motor-part.js#L155)

The virtual breaker latches when the **signed drive-gear** speed is at least 20 rad/s. It sets source torque to zero via the latch and raises output damping to 5. The rotor can coast or be back-driven. Reset clears the latch, sets output damping to **0** and wakes the three bodies. The pawl is visual and there is no charge or depletion state. The gear ratio would put 20 rad/s at 156 rad/s on the output before clamping, so the trip test uses the slower gear directly, and the per-body cap prevents converting the trip condition to an exact post-step output speed. Upper Story's instructions say a newly placed battery trips when unloaded. In a local 1,000-step run of this checkout's unloaded battery class, its output and intermediate gears reached the rotation cap and the drive gear plateaued at 19.85 rad/s, just below the trip threshold. ### Resistor

A sealed rotary damper shears silicone oil. Upper Story varies the oil viscosity to set the resistance and specifies ±10% at a reference spin voltage for its variety pack. The oil's non-Newtonian behavior makes physical resistance depend on applied spin voltage. The ideal reference law is $V=RI$. [Upper Story resistor specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-resistor-100-1000-variety-pack) · [Upper Story units](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science)

In the simulator $r=0.041168/2$ m, $d=R/5$, $\rho=d/100=R/500$, and $J=\pi\rho r^4/2$. $R$ is the numeric Ω label. Available labels are 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 2000 Ω, with 1000 Ω the default. A starting angular impulse of `0.000001` is applied before the constructor sets the final density. The shaft has inertia proportional to $R$, and the steady torque coefficient seen by a chain is $Jd/(1+hd)\propto R^2/(1+R/150)$ at $h=1/30$. Thus the torque law is linear in speed at fixed label, and the coefficient varies nonlinearly across label settings. The simulation includes viscous numerical damping and omits the oil's voltage dependence. ### Capacitor and voltmeter

Turning the kit sprocket winds a torsion spring. Angle represents charge, opposing torque represents voltage, and stored energy can drive the chain back as the spring unwinds. The current product is 1.2 spin mF and its gauge reads −9 to +9 spin V. A capacitor disconnected from the chain can unwind freely. The ideal reference law is $V=Q/C$, with $I=dQ/dt$. [Upper Story capacitor specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-capacitor-1-2-mf) · [Mould transcript, 4:10–5:16 and 9:37–10:01](https://lilys.ai/ko/notes/638148)

In the simulator, $r=0.041168/2$ m, $\rho=0.1$, and $d=5$. Let $a=288^\circ=1.6\pi$ rad and $C$ be the numeric F label. The applied spring torque is

\tau_P=-\frac{\theta}{a}\frac{0.001}{C}(0.0000185256\times2.5).
$$

The nominal interval $[-a,a]$ normalizes the law. The revolute joint has limits disabled, and the spring formula extrapolates beyond them. Values are 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10, and 50 mF, with 1 mF the default. [parts/capacitor-part.js:4–12,109–142,194–195,215–236](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/capacitor-part.js#L215)

### Inductor

A flywheel stores energy in rotation. Torque changes its angular speed, so the chain must pull to change current. The physical part is rated 55 spin H. Three steel balls at the rim also act as flyweights for a high-speed brake. The ideal reference law is $V=L\,dI/dt$. ### Junction

The physical differential has a sun (top), ring (middle) and planet carrier (bottom). The sprocket diameters and gear ratios give the tooth-speed relation $v_b=v_m+v_t$ with consistent signed directions. With the bottom feeding the two branches this becomes $I_b=I_m+I_t$, Kirchhoff's current law. For an ideal lossless differential, power balance with two independent branch currents gives $V_b=V_m=V_t$, with (V_b) signed as input and the others as outputs. The same component splits supply and recombines returns. Leaving a branch sprocket free allows it to turn and shunt the useful drive. [Upper Story junction specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-junction-value-pack) · [Upper Story parallel-circuit diagram and return path](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science) · [Mould transcript, 9:37–10:01](https://lilys.ai/ko/notes/638148)

The simulator uses a helper rotor $n$ with the same radius as $b$. A ratio $-1$ gear enforces $\omega_n=\omega_b$. Another gear, ratio $1/2$, acts on the middle rotor relative to the helper and the top rotor relative to the bottom, so

$$
(\omega_m-\omega_n)+\tfrac12(\omega_t-\omega_b)=0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 3\omega_b=2\omega_m+\omega_t.
$$

Planck gets the actual endpoints from the two revolute joints. The chain radii $r_b,r_m,r_t$ are 0.033941, 0.022638, and 0.011348 m, giving the exact constraint $I_b=[2r_b/(3r_m)]I_m+[r_b/(3r_t)]I_t\approx0.99953I_m+0.99697I_t$ before solver error. The small mismatch from exact KCL follows from the radii and internal gear ratios. The lossless effort ratios would carry the same weighting factors, since input power splits between the two branch powers. [parts/junction-part.js:4–9,99–110,140–161](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/junction-part.js#L140) · [planck.js:9983–10045,10151–10155,10192–10222](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.js#L9983)

All three port rotors use `ρ=0.1` and `d=0.5`. The auxiliary bottom-sized rotor has `d=0`, and a cap rotor of radius 0.0045 m and density 0.1 is welded to it. A starting impulse of `0.0000002` is applied to the bottom rotor. These inertias and damping load the ports. The active constraints stand in for the planetary gear teeth. [parts/junction-part.js:4–9,117–161,192](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/junction-part.js#L117) · [planck.js:189–191,14022–14026](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.js#L14022)

### Diode

The physical ratchet passes clockwise rotation and blocks reverse rotation. Upper Story gives a forward threshold of about 0.7 spin V and puts reverse breakdown above the chain's rated maximum. Which way current passes relative to a source depends on the chain wrap. [Upper Story diode specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-diode-value-pack) · [Upper Story science guide](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science)

The simulator uses $r=0.033979/2$ m, $\rho=0.1$, $d=0.05$, and an initial impulse of `0.0000002`. The revolute joint starts with default zero limits. After each step the running maximum $m_n=\max(m_{n-1},\theta_n)$ is initialized or advanced, and the next allowed interval is $[m_n,m_n+2\pi]$. This approximates one-way travel with a moving stop. Forward torque only has to overcome the model's inertia and damping. There is no 0.7 V threshold, ratchet tooth pitch, or reverse-breakdown law. Planck's limit solver allows angular slop of 2° in position correction. [parts/diode-part.js:4,40–68,84–98](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/diode-part.js#L84) · [planck.js:3275,11960–11966,12388–12415](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/planck.js#L12388)

### Transistor

The physical part is a voltage-controlled brake. Gate torque turns a spring-loaded top sprocket, moving the mechanism that releases clamps from the path sprocket's rubber ring. The gate and path are separate loops. Upper Story specifies about 1.5 spin V for fully on and 2.2 millispin coulombs of gate charge. The kit permits either gate polarity. The useful circuit analogy is a FET-style variable resistance controlled by the voltage across its isolated gate. [Upper Story science guide and transfer curve](https://upperstory.com/en/spintronics/science) · [Upper Story transistor specifications](https://store.upperstory.com/collections/spintronic-parts/products/spintronic-transistor-value-pack) · [Mould transcript, 10:07–10:45](https://lilys.ai/ko/notes/638148)

In the simulator level 3 is the gate ($r_g=0.063772/2$ m, $\rho_g=0.1$, $d_g=0.02$). Level 1 is the path ($r_p=0.03398/2$ m), and level 2 is absent. Put $s=+1$ for the clockwise property and $-1$ for its default, counterclockwise. The gate's signed angle $s\theta_g$ is constrained between $a=2\pi(0.018)=6.48^\circ$ and $b=2\pi(0.125)=45^\circ$. The spring takes the raw travel fraction $u=(s\theta_g-a)/(b-a)$ and applies $\tau_{P,g}=-s\,u(0.0000047829\times2.5)$. Only the brake uses the clamped fraction $q=\min(1,\max(0,u))$. The simulated gate sweep corresponds to about 2.14 millispin coulombs from $r_g(b-a)/10$. [parts/transistor-part.js:4–7,135–165,251–291,315–344](../ref/spintronics-simulator/public/parts/transistor-part.js#L267)

The path's angular damping is $d_p=\max(0.5,100^{2(1-q)}-1)$. Each update also sets $\rho_p=d_p/100$ and recomputes $J_p=\pi\rho_p r_p^4/2$. Thus off is strongly damped $d_p=9999$, while on retains a damping floor of 0.5. This brake law is symmetric in path rotation, has finite off-state drag instead of a lock, and changes path inertia with gate voltage. The gate has a spring, inertia, and hard stops. The rendered brake pieces and balls do not transmit forces to the path. There is no body diode or MOSFET channel-current saturation law. ### Ammeter (`phonograph`)

In the kit, a wedge attached to a diaphragm rides over a ridged moving surface. Each bump drives the diaphragm once, so for $N$ evenly spaced bumps encountered per sprocket revolution the repetition rate is $f=N|\omega|/(2\pi)$ Hz. The horn makes it audible. Pitch conveys current magnitude. The bump count and a calibration of loudness are **unverified**. The source calls the simulator class `PhonographPart` and the editor labels it Ammeter. A separate kit phonograph is **unverified**. ### Level changer

This part couples shafts across heights for chain routing. Upper Story's forum explains that it is a simulator part and that an ON switch performs the same function in the physical kit. With equal radii and equal wrap orientation, the coupling carries the same signed chain speed across levels. It can transfer torque at zero speed as well. Its ideal electrical abstraction is a lossless 1:1 coupling. ### Base tile and tile connector

### 457 · Parallel resistors

The published layout shows 500 Ω and 1000 Ω load loops on a junction, with the battery feeding the trunk. Each resistor loop has the same available voltage in the ideal junction model. In the ideal 6 V calculation, $I_{500}=12$ mA, $I_{1000}=6$ mA, $I_b=18$ mA, and $R_\mathrm{eq}=333.3\ldots\ \Omega$. Hold one branch stationary and its current drops to zero. ### 459 · Sinusoidal oscillator

Upper Story identifies the output as sinusoidal, and a builder identifies the simulator-page circuit as a Colpitts oscillator. In that reading, $L$ and the series combination of $C_1$ and $C_2$ form the tank. Its energy alternates between flywheel motion and spring deflection. The capacitor tap feeds a fraction of the oscillation back to the active stage in phase, and the powered transistor replenishes losses. The ideal calculation uses $C_\mathrm{eq}=C_1 C_2/(C_1+C_2)$ and $f_0=(2\pi\sqrt{LC_\mathrm{eq}})^{-1}$. For scale, using the simulator's 50 H and 1 mF defaults for $L$ and both capacitors gives $C_\mathrm{eq}=0.5$ mF and an ideal $f_0\approx1.01$ Hz. Increasing either tank capacitance or the flywheel inertia lowers the ideal resonant frequency. Exact component values, feedback-chain routing, and simulated frequency are **unverified**. ### 460 · Ring oscillator

Upper Story reports three transistors switching in sequence. In circuit terms an odd ring of inverting stages feeds the final output back to the first input. An increase at one gate releases its path brake. With an inverting load connection the stage output falls, and the following gate responds after a delay. Tracking one rising transition around three inversions returns a falling transition, and a second trip restores the rising transition. With equal effective stage delay $t_d$, the estimate is $T\approx6t_d$. Gate travel and rotor dynamics supply delay in the model. The saved stage wiring, delay, and period are **unverified**. ### 461 · Pulse generator

Closing the switch gives one audible burst, then the ammeter slows toward silence even while the switch stays closed. Electrically that is a step-to-pulse response. Charging a capacitor permits transient current, while the charged capacitor balances a DC drive with its spring torque. For a simple RC equivalent with an initially discharged capacitor, a step $V_0$ gives $I(t)=(V_0/R)e^{-t/(RC)}$, so the transient has fallen to $1/e$ of its initial current after $RC$. With an ammeter in the transient path, pitch falls with the magnitude of current. Upper Story also publishes a physical pulse-generator diagram. A community redraw of that diagram identifies a capacitor branch and a 200 Ω resistor. The exact RC reduction, roles of individual timing branches, and pulse duration in the hosted save are **unverified**.
